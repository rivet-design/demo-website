We’ve found a way as humans to invent tools to design the world around us since our earliest days.

But some thinkers have asked what happens when you go beyond tools and the machine works as a genuine design partner to you instead.

Looking at this history of design helps explain why Rivet exists today.

<SplitImageText
  src="/images/about/leonardo-sketches.webp"
  alt="Leonardo da Vinci sketches of mechanical inventions on aged paper"
>
  In medieval times, the makers of the time wrote on parchment. Because
  parchment was expensive and scarce, it was hard to try ideas.

Paper became abundant before the Renaissance. Early designers like Leonardo
now had notebooks to fill with ideas. Because it got cheaper to explore a
design, more ideas flourished. Parchment was the original design tool—a blank
canvas that invited the owner to pour their ideas into.

</SplitImageText>

<SplitImageText
  src="/images/about/drafting-studio.webp"
  alt="Designers working at drafting tables in a mid-century design studio"
  format="square"
>
  Tools kept progressing. Drafting tables of the 1960s. The earliest
  computer-aided design programs of the ’70s. The beginnings of modern design
  software with Photoshop, then Sketch and Figma.
</SplitImageText>

<SplitImageText
  src="/images/about/design-vellum.webp"
  alt="A package of translucent design vellum used for tracing and drawing"
  format="square"
>
  Yesterday’s and today’s tools are closer than you might think. Modern tools
  like Figma are like digital parchment. They’re flexible and expressive, but
  it’s dependent on you to fill in. They don’t understand you. They can’t
  challenge or augment your thinking.

Since the earliest computers came out, a small group of thinkers have asked a
different question of our design tools instead: what if they could act as a
partner to you? **What if you, the designer, had a partner that helped you
explore infinitely more ideas?**

</SplitImageText>

Engineers already have their own version of this today. They’ve become less like individual engineers and more like architects. An engineer now has a team of helpful agents that helps them implement wildly more than they could on their own.

Today, designers have to leave great ideas unexplored. There just isn’t enough time to go prototype each interesting direction.

<PullQuote>
  A designer with Rivet becomes more like a creative director.
</PullQuote>

Rivet is a different way of designing that helps you explore dozens of ideas. It takes the time to understand your references and aesthetics, explores on your behalf, and then composes tools with you to refine your interface.

Why Rivet? A rivet is a small connective piece of metal that joins two materials together. They’re composable—thousands or even millions of rivets form remarkably complex structures. It felt like the right word to name a company that wants to join designer and machine.

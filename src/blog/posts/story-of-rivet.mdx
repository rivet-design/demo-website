In medieval times, the makers of the time wrote on parchment. Because parchment was expensive and scarce, it was hard to try ideas.

Paper became abundant before the Renaissance. Early designers like Leonardo now had notebooks to fill with ideas. Because it got cheaper to explore a design, more ideas flourished.

Designers in the ’60s didn’t have software to make for yet, but they needed to invent all sorts of physical products to bring into the world. Without computers, they sketched ideas with drafting tables and tracing paper. It was time-consuming work that demanded precision.

Yesterday’s and today’s tools share the same limitation: **they don’t help you explore the design space.** That’s why we’re building Rivet. What if you, the designer, had a tool that helped you explore infinitely more ideas?

Engineers already have their own version of this today. They’ve become less like individual engineers and more like architects. An engineer who used to write each line of code by hand now has a team of helpful agents that helps them implement wildly more than they could on their own.

Today, you might have six different tools for design open: Figma, Claude, Cursor, and even your own personal tools that you’ve made to plug in the gaps. Rivet is a different way of designing that understands your references, explores on your behalf, and then helps you compose tools to refine and select iterations.

A rivet is a small connective piece of metal that joins two materials together. They’re composable—thousands or even millions of rivets form remarkably complex structures. It felt like the right word to name a company that wants to join design and code.
